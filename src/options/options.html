FactCheck

Keys are stored locally on this device and are only ever sent to the provider you select below. This extension has no server of its own.

This is a cross-checking aid, not a source of fact. A flagged sentence is not a false one, search results are not verification, and AI summaries can be confidently wrong. Read the sources and judge for yourself. Provided as is, with no warranty and no liability accepted.

In one paragraph

While you read, this extension marks sentences that state a fact worth checking. It does not decide whether they are true. When you want to know, you press a button and it goes looking for what other sources say.

The marking happens on your own computer and is free. Nothing is searched, and nothing is sent anywhere, until you press something.

What you will see

On the page, a marked sentence looks like this:

The mayor said the new bridge cost 40 million dollars more than planned and that traffic has fallen since it opened.

Only the first part is marked, because only that part states a checkable fact. Click a mark, or open the side panel, and you get a card for it:

Check-worthiness 0.82
The new bridge cost 40 million dollars more than planned.

Those are pictures of the buttons, not the buttons themselves. The real ones are in the side panel, which opens when you click the extension's icon.

What each button does

Search in browser Opens a search for the claim in your normal search engine, in a new tab. It does not paste the whole sentence in: it sends the figure and the names in quotation marks plus the words that pin the topic, which is what you would type yourself. Tell it which engine you use, under Search in Settings, and the query is shaped to suit that engine. No key, no limit. This is the main button until you add a search key.
Add background Looks the claim up in Wikipedia and shows what it finds under the claim, along with any published fact-check. Good for who someone is, what a term means, or what a figure usually looks like. It will not have this week's news.
Check sources Replaces the two above once you add a web-search key: real results from the open web, inside the panel, with dates, source tiers, differing figures and the rest of the evidence reading. One search call per press.
Check with AI Finds the sources, then has an AI read them and tell you what they say. Only appears once you have set up an AI in Settings.
Rescan Reads the page again. Press this after you change a setting, otherwise the change only affects the next page you open.
👍 👎 Always check this website, or never check it. Your choice sticks.
Right-click Highlight the sentence first, by dragging across it with the mouse, then right-click the highlighted text and choose "Fact-check the highlighted text". The menu item only appears when text is selected. Use this when something was not marked and you think it should have been.
Alt+Shift+N Jump to the next marked sentence, the way Ctrl+F jumps between matches. Alt+Shift+P goes back.

Reading the results

After you press Check sources, each claim gets a short summary of what was found. It is built from things that can be pointed at, not from an opinion:

  • whether the sources are actually about the same thing;
  • whether they give a different number from the claim;
  • whether they are old enough to be about the wrong year;
  • what kind of websites they are;
  • whether a real fact-checking organisation has already ruled on it.

The panel writes these out in plain words underneath, so you can see why it said what it said, and disagree with it.

A mark never means "this is false". It means "this is the kind of sentence worth checking". The judgment stays yours.

Do I need an API key?

No. It works without one. A key just widens where it can look.

With no keyMarks claims, searches Wikipedia, and opens your normal search engine in a tab. Free, no accounts.
With a search keyFull web results appear inside the panel, including recent news that Wikipedia will not have.
With a fact-check keyAlso looks up verdicts already published by fact-checking organisations. Free from Google.
With an AIAdds a plain-language summary of what the sources say, and flags where they disagree.

A "key" is a long password a website gives you so a program can use its service on your behalf. You paste it into Settings once. It is stored on this computer and sent only to the service it belongs to.

Where to get one

Web search Brave Search API is the one to try first: make an account, choose the free plan, copy the key. SerpAPI is the alternative and returns Google results. Both have free allowances that change over time, so check their pricing pages rather than trusting a number here.
Published fact-checks Google Fact Check Tools. Free. Enable the API, then create a key under Credentials. Most sentences have never been fact-checked, so this finds nothing more often than not, and is worth a lot when it does.
AI Anthropic or OpenAI, both paid per use and usually fractions of a penny per summary. Or run one on your own machine for nothing, which is explained below.

Nothing is ever spent without you pressing a button. There is no background activity, so a key cannot quietly drain while you browse.

Running the AI on your own computer

"Local model" means the AI runs on your machine instead of on a company's servers. It is free, it works offline, and what you are reading never leaves your computer. The trade is that it uses your own processor and memory, and small models are less sharp than the large paid ones.

There are two ways to have one, and neither needs an account.

1. The model built into your browser

Recent versions of Chrome ship a small AI model. If yours has it, "Your browser's built-in AI" appears in the Settings list. Choose it and you are done. There is nothing to install and nothing to pay. Chrome downloads the model the first time you use it, so the first summary is slow and later ones are quick. Brave does not offer this to extensions, so use the option below there.

2. Ollama, on your machine

Ollama is a free program that runs AI models locally. Setting it up takes about five minutes:

  1. Download and install Ollama from ollama.com.
  2. Open a terminal and run ollama pull llama3.1. This fetches the model, a few gigabytes, once.
  3. Leave Ollama running. It serves at http://localhost:11434, which is already filled in under Settings.
  4. In Settings, set the AI provider to "Local model", and the model name to llama3.1.

That is the whole setup. The extension then sends the claim and the search results to the program running on your own computer, and no request leaves the machine. LM Studio and anything else that speaks the same format work the same way; just point the endpoint at it.

CostNothing, ever. No account, no key, no bill.
PrivacyNothing leaves your computer. This is the strongest option if that matters to you.
SpeedSeconds on a recent machine, slower on an older one. It uses your processor while it works.
QualityGood enough to summarise a handful of sources. The paid models are better at spotting subtle disagreement.
Disk spaceA few gigabytes for the model, downloaded once.

Websites that are never checked

Some pages are nobody's business, including this extension's. Banking, health, email, and account pages are never read, and neither is any page showing a password or a card field. On those pages the extension does nothing at all: nothing is marked, nothing is stored, and the right-click option refuses too.

You can add your own sites to that list in Settings, and the thumbs buttons in the panel let you block or allow a site as you come across it.

Making it look how you want

Under Appearance in Settings you can change the colour of the marks, use a plain underline instead of a tint, make the underline thinner or thicker, enlarge the side panel text, or hide the markers that appear over YouTube videos. There is a live sample there that changes as you choose.

If you would rather nothing appeared on the page at all, choose "Nothing on the page, panel only". Claims are still found and still listed, and you can still jump between them.

Search

Wikipedia works the moment the extension is installed and needs no key. SerpAPI or Brave Search give full web results in the panel and need one. Every claim also has a "Search in browser" button that opens your default search engine, whichever you use, with no key at all.
This shapes the query the "Search in browser" button sends, because engines do not read one the same way. Google, Bing and Yahoo take two phrases in quotation marks; DuckDuckGo and Brave call their own operators experimental and return more when only the figure is quoted; Perplexity and other answer engines want a plain question instead of keywords. Leaving this on your browser's default sends the careful version that suits all of them, since an extension cannot see which engine you set. Naming one here also opens that engine directly.
Saved on this device until you change it. Claims are flagged for free; a search call is only spent when you press "Check sources" on a claim in the side panel.
Strips lead-in attributions such as "officials said that" and, for long sentences, searches only the distinctive words. Off until you have checked it helps: compare both forms on your own claims. That page uses the key saved here and caches results, so it spends a few search calls the first time and none after.
Adds academic terms to web searches and lists journal, university and science-agency results first. Also asks OpenAlex, a free scholarly index that needs no key, for peer-reviewed articles when you press "Check sources", shown in their own section with journal, year and citations. The claim is sent to OpenAlex only when you press that button.

Reading the sources

A search snippet is about 150 characters and often cuts off the number the claim turns on. With this on, the top two results are fetched and the paragraphs that mention the claim are used instead. The requests are sent without cookies, so they are anonymous, and they only happen when you press Check sources.

Remembered answers

The same claim checked twice, or two articles about the same event, then costs one call instead of two. Answers are kept on this machine only, in the browser's own storage, and include the text of the claims you checked.

Published fact-checks (optional)

Looks for verdicts already published by fact-checking organisations such as Snopes, PolitiFact and Full Fact, and shows them with the search results when you press "Check sources".

Training data: the ClaimBuster dataset

The model that decides what is worth checking is trained on the ClaimBuster dataset, created by the IDIR Lab at the University of Texas at Arlington. It is a corpus of sentences from United States presidential debates, each labelled by human annotators as a check-worthy factual claim, an unimportant factual sentence, or not a factual sentence at all.

  • Project: idir.uta.edu/claimbuster
  • Dataset: zenodo.org/records/3836810, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.3609356
  • Licence: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International, which permits redistribution and derivative works, including commercial use, on condition that the source is credited

A model trained on that data is a derivative work of it, so the credit travels inside model.json as well as sitting here and in ATTRIBUTION.md. The raw data is not redistributed with this project; it is downloaded from the Zenodo record above.

Papers the dataset authors ask to be cited

What the labels mean, and do not

ClaimBuster labels whether a sentence is worth checking, never whether it is true. The classifier inherits exactly that scope. Its scores are not truth ratings and are not presented as any.
